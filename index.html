Garry Kasparov was world champion from 1985 to 2000 and is widely considered one of the greatest players ever. In 1996 he played IBM's chess computer and won. In May 1997 they played a rematch. Who won?

Turning point: Game 2 and Game 6

In Game 2, Deep Blue made a quiet, unexpected positional move. Kasparov was shaken and resigned. Analysts later found he could have forced a draw by perpetual check. He suspected IBM of cheating, and asked for printouts of the computer's logs, which IBM refused.

Why it matters today

Brute force then, learning now. Deep Blue searched about 200 million positions per second using custom chess chips and rules written by humans. In 2016, AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol at Go by learning from data and self-play, with no hand-written strategy.

Humans plus machines. After the loss, Kasparov promoted "Advanced Chess", where humans and computers team up. Today a free phone app plays stronger chess than any human ever has.

The "intelligence" question. Was Deep Blue intelligent, or just fast? We still ask this about modern AI.

Ask the class: Which game will AI beat us at next, and does it count if the machine doesn't understand it?

1-page summary

May 1997, New York: the first time a computer beat a reigning world chess champion in a match.

Hook

Kasparov won in 1996 (4–2). A year later, an upgraded Deep Blue won 3.5–2.5.

Context

IBM's machine searched about 200 million positions per second, tuned with help from grandmasters.

Turning point

Game 2: Kasparov resigned a position he could have drawn, and suspected cheating. Game 6 ended in 19 moves.

Why it matters today

Brute-force search gave way to learning systems like AlphaGo (2016). Humans and machines now play as teams.
